01

A letter from the editorOn jargon, and what design is actually for.

P. 04 — Prose

I don't do jargon. I never have. If you've ever received a deck from me that used the phrase "orchestrate design revolutions," I owe you an apology and possibly a refund. This issue is an attempt to do the opposite — to show the work, plainly, and let it stand on its own.

I'm a self-taught Product/UX designer. I have a Computer Engineering degree I don't use much, and a small library of opinions about research, handoff, and what makes a product feel finished. I've spent the last five years helping early- and growth-stage teams take products from zero to one, and explaining to clients, plainly, why that work matters. The features in this issue are the ones I'd pitch first.

"I design products that users love, for brands that care. That's it."

When I'm not doing that, I'm with my dog Jerry. He's the kind of collaborator who doesn't write copy but does walk across the keyboard at exactly the wrong moment. He's listed as co-editor. You'll find his notes throughout.

Read what you want. Skip what you don't. If something strikes you as interesting — or if you'd like to talk about a project of your own — the colophon at the back of the issue has the addresses.

Feature 02 / Dealer portal Thence · 2021
rebuilt, end-to-end
p. 08Asian Paints

A dealer portal that dealers would use.

Twelve screens, one ugly problem, and a category of users who would rather call a sales rep than log in.

The setup. Asian Paints dealers were calling sales reps to place every order. Competitor portals existed; dealers hated them. Transaction volumes and AOV were flat. We had a brief: improve the UX, digitize ordering, and nudge AOV up.

"Make it so fast and so good that calling the sales rep becomes the weird thing to do."

The work. Heuristic evaluation, then a full redesign of twelve screens: Home, Trending, Categories, Catalog, Listing, Order, Stock, Wishlist, Cart, Tracking, Account, Reports. Added barcode-scan ordering, personalized recommendations from history, loyalty points, and order-size incentives. Worked alongside a UI designer, PM, design lead, and the client team.

The result. A +20% lift in online transaction volume and a healthy increase in user engagement and loyalty. Salesman-dealer interaction dropped. The portal started carrying its weight.

Feature 03 / Onboarding Thence · 2021
mismatch, found
p. 14Boost360

The uninstall wasn't a bug. It was a promise.

80% of new users were gone before they signed up. We diagnosed the cause in week one, redesigned the flow, and watched the metric move.

The setup. 80% of new Boost360 users uninstalled the app before signing up. Average time-to-signup was 30 minutes. The brief was to find out why, and fix it.

What it wasn't. Crashes. OTP. Permissions. Ads. None of the usual suspects. The clue was in the marketing channels — users were arriving expecting one product and meeting another.

"It was a UX problem hiding inside a positioning problem."

The fix. Made signup visual. Communicated USPs during onboarding, not after. Added social proof for trust. Created a quick win before the form fields. Routed users to support if they got stuck. Aligned marketing with the product at every touchpoint.

The result. −60% uninstall rate within 14 days. A significant reduction in time-to-signup. Users described the new flow as "easy," "fast," and "motivating" in usability testing — adjectives the old flow never earned.

p. 20Wikipedia / KaiOS

What a feature phone teaches you about design.

25 in-person interviews across three cities, in 2020, to learn how people who chose not to use a smartphone use Wikipedia anyway.

The setup. Wikimedia wanted to understand the people using Wikipedia on JioPhones — a smart feature phone running KaiOS. These were people who had chosen not to use a smartphone. That choice was the entire question.

The fieldwork. 25 in-person interviews, 45–60 minutes each, in Pune, Mumbai, and Delhi. Recruited participants, ran the sessions, transcribed, built personas, mapped journeys, and produced a research report. We sat with people in their homes, watched them use their phones, and asked why.

"The phone was the point — not a compromise. Designing for it meant starting over."

What we learned. Why JioPhone: cheap to maintain, durable, good battery, familiar keypad, family on the Jio network, smart features at an affordable price. What they wanted from Wikipedia: more images and video, English numerals in Hindi articles, simpler words, a search bar inside articles, a news section, the ability to copy text.

What we recommended. Voice-first interaction. Readability for small screens. Video content. Better search visibility. Regional-language SEO. Simpler Hindi. The findings shaped how Wikipedia approached KaiOS for the year that followed.

03

Interview: four working beliefsOn consistency, curiosity, function, and giving a damn.

P. 26 — Q&A
The prompt

You list four principles on your site. They're short. Walk us through them.

I keep them short on purpose. A principle you can't say in one breath isn't one you'll remember at 2am when the deadline is fake and the scope is real.

Principle 01 · Effort

"Consistency beats talent."

I'm not the most talented designer in the room. I am, reliably, in the room. Consistent effort compounds into results that look like talent in hindsight — and I think that's a better bet to make. The boring version of this is "show up." The less boring version is "show up, and bring better questions than you brought yesterday."

Principle 02 · Method

"Let curiosity guide you."

I know a little about a lot of things, and it helps me connect dots between scattered information. Each project is a journey of discovery. Curiosity is what keeps the work making a difference past the kickoff slide — and what makes me genuinely hard to bore on a long engagement.

Principle 03 · Order of ops

"Form follows function."

Problems are solved with functionality, not aesthetics. The visual layer is what creates delight, but only once the functionality is right. The order matters. A beautiful app that doesn't solve a real problem is a poster. A functional app with a thoughtful visual layer is a product.

Principle 04 · Stance

"Give a damn."

I work with a founder's mindset. I take ownership of outcomes, not deliverables. I treat the project's success as my success while it's mine. That means showing up worried about things that aren't on the brief — because the brief is the floor, not the ceiling.
